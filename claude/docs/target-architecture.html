Target architecture

Target architecture: one customer identity across four systems

Lumen Health case study. The design that replaces the guesswork in legacy-sync, written against the findings in how legacy-sync works today.

Every design choice below answers a failure in the current system. Where the brief leaves something open, the assumption is written down in the last section.

Summary

A small identity service sits between the commercial systems and the clinical systems. It gives every real person one opaque person_key and links each record in e-commerce, the CRM, the portal and the EHR to that key with a confidence score and the evidence behind it. Links the service is sure of are made automatically. Uncertain ones go to a review queue for a person to decide. Nothing is ever guessed from a surname and a zip code again.

Each kit carries three roles, buyer, registrant and patient, so a gift or a family purchase no longer has to be forced onto one CRM account. A kit registration is accepted the moment the customer submits it: the service creates a provisional person and resolves it afterwards. Contact details keep their history instead of being overwritten, consent is recorded per channel and the strictest setting survives a merge. The CRM and marketing receive the key, the stage of each kit and consent. Nothing clinical crosses to them.

One keyper person, added beside every system's own IDs, never replacing them
Three rolesper kit: buyer, registrant, patient, which may be three people

Topology

The identity service is a small, separately deployed component with four parts: a resolve API that systems call, a matcher, a review queue and an append-only event log, over one store. It is not inside the CRM, because the CRM is being replaced. It is not inside the portal, because the portal must never wait on it.

Blue components are new or changed. E-commerce, the portal, the EHR and the lab keep their current behaviour; each gains a field for the person key and an event to the identity service.

Matching and uncertainty

Deterministic links first, scored pairs second, people last. The thresholds are set for precision: a wrong merge exposes one person's health information to another, a missed link costs a review.

Deterministic links

  • Registration to chart through the lab result for the same kit barcode. In the sample all 406 results carry the registration's name and birthdate.
  • Order to opportunity through ecom_order_id and order to kit through order_id. These link records, not people: the buyer is the order's customer, the registrant is whoever registered the kit.
  • ecom_customer_id on a CRM account is treated as weak evidence, not a key. The sync stamped it onto relatives' accounts.

Candidate pairs

Two records are compared only when they share a normalised email, a normalised phone, a surname and zip, or a surname and birthdate. In the sample this keeps the comparisons in the low thousands instead of the two million possible pairs.

Scoring

FieldAgreeDisagreeNote
Email, exactStrong positiveNoneMany people have several addresses, so disagreement says nothing
Email, dotted variant or same mailboxPositiveNone
PhonePositiveNone39 phones are shared by two e-commerce customers, so it is weaker than email
Birthdate, both presentStrong positiveVetoTwo known birthdates that differ are different people, with no exception for a likely typo. A mistyped birthdate costs a review

Street and zipSmall positiveNoneAgreement means the same household, which is not the same person
Known relationshipStrong negativeTwo people already recorded as a household or a gift pair stay two people

Weights follow the Fellegi-Sunter form: the log ratio of how often a field agrees for true matches against how often it agrees by chance. In the working slice the weights are set by hand, so its scores rank pairs and are not probabilities.

Validation before any automatic link is trusted

  • The unit is the automatic link: a record pair at or above the threshold. The population is every such pair.
  • A simple random sample of that population is labelled independently by two reviewers, with a third settling disagreements. The slice draws this sample with a fixed seed.
  • The false-match rate is reported with a 95 percent Wilson interval, which is valid because the sample is random and complete. The upper bound is held against the gate, never the point estimate.
  • The rollout gate asks for at least 600 labelled automatic links. With no false match in 600 the upper bound is about 0.6 percent. The acceptable bound is a decision for the privacy officer.
  • Samples of the review queue and of pairs just under the threshold are labelled too. They estimate reviewer workload and missed links and say nothing about automatic-link precision.
  • Rule families that have not been measured stay in review whatever they score. Today that covers addresses that match only after a plus tag is removed. Phone numbers with a non-US country code match only on every digit.
  • Weights are refitted from review decisions. The sample is redrawn and relabelled after each refit.

Thresholds

  • 0.90 and above: link automatically, record evidence, raise an event. The 0.90 figure is provisional until the validation above has been run. Until then the service runs in shadow and writes nothing.
  • 0.60 to 0.90: create a review item. Both records remain separate people until decided.
  • Below 0.60: no link, no item.

Clustering

Accepted links are grouped into people with two constraints. A person may not hold two different birthdates. Two EHR charts are never merged by code; a chart pair that scores as one person goes to clinical operations as a review item. Transitive closure runs only across automatic and human-accepted links, never across review items. A record's confidence is the weakest link on its path into the cluster.

What happens while a pair is under review

  • Both records stay separate people. A registration keeps its provisional person, so the kit, its chart and its result remain attached to the registrant and results are shown without delay.
  • The CRM shows the account with a pending-review flag. Nothing is moved.
  • Marketing reads kit stage, which does not depend on the review, so the registrant is not asked to register again.
  • Review items carry reason codes such as same surname and zip, birthdate missing on one side, email shared by two first names. A reviewer sees the two records side by side inside the restricted zone and decides same, different or cannot tell.
  • Every decision is an event and becomes a labelled pair for the next calibration.

Traps the sample data teaches

  • Eight registration emails are used by more than one person and five EHR emails sit on more than one chart. A shared email is a household signal, not an identity.
  • Twenty non-gift kits were registered by the buyer under a second email. Work and personal addresses for one person are common, so a new email must not create a new person when phone, birthdate and name agree.
  • Same-name relatives with different birthdates exist. The birthdate veto handles them where a birthdate is present. Where the CRM has none, the pair goes to review.
  • Consecutive e-commerce customer IDs often belong to one person. That is how the sample was generated, not evidence. The matcher must not use it.

The working slice is the reference implementation of this section. It reads data/, writes identities.csv and review_queue.csv and prints the precision estimate against the labelled pairs.

Human review

Review is the second half of matching, not an exception path. The matcher links what it is sure of and hands everything else to a person. Review is asynchronous by design: nothing a customer does ever waits for a reviewer. Every system has a defined behaviour while an item is open.

This section is a design. The working slice produces the queue as a file and implements none of the workflow below.

The random audit is how the false-match rate is measured and kept measured. A fixed share of automatic links, 2 percent to start, is drawn at random each day and reviewed blind, without the matcher's score on screen. The labelled sample used before go-live is the first batch of this audit. The audit never stops.

An item that passes twice its age target is raised to the head of operations. No item expires into a decision: silence never merges and never closes. The targets are proposals, to be set with whoever staffs the queue.

Safeguards

  • Least exposure. The queue lists items by ID, score and reason code. Names, birthdates and contact details appear only on the comparison screen for the item a reviewer has claimed. That read is logged. Result values are never shown.
  • Blind audit. Audit items hide the matcher's score and decision, so the reviewer is not led.
  • Two-person rule for merges that touch clinical records, as set out above. The second reviewer does not see the first decision.
  • No self-review. A reviewer cannot decide an item about their own record or one they reported.
  • Stale-decision check. When a decision is applied, the service compares the evidence the reviewer saw with the evidence now. If a record, link or birthdate changed in between, the decision is not applied and the item is superseded.
  • Constraints still hold. A human decision cannot create a person with two EHR charts merged by code. A merge that would put two known birthdates on one person requires the reviewer to mark which is the error. It is then routed to a lead.
  • Reversible. Every merge can be split from the event log. Nothing is deleted.
  • Reviewer quality. A share of closed items is re-reviewed by a second person. Agreement is tracked per reviewer.
  • No bulk decisions. There is no approve-all. Decisions are rate-limited per reviewer.

What review feeds back

  • Every closed item is a labelled pair. The audit items give the false-match rate of automatic links, with its interval, on a dashboard that the rollout gates read.
  • Weights are refitted from the labels on a schedule. After a refit the thresholds are re-validated on a fresh random sample before the new weights go live.
  • A reason-code family whose reviewed items come back the same person nearly every time is a candidate for automatic linking. One that comes back different people is a candidate for a stronger negative weight.
  • Measures: queue size and age by priority, decisions per day, share decided same, reviewer agreement, superseded share, apply failures, false-match rate from the audit, wrong links reported.

Registration without waiting

The brief's fixed constraint. The portal stores the registration and answers the customer before anything else happens. If the service is down, events queue and the only thing delayed is the CRM's view of who registered.

How it treats protected health information

The brief says to treat the data as protected health information. The identity store holds demographics tied to test kits and results, so it is inside the boundary and is treated as a clinical system for access, logging and contracts.

Two zones inside the boundary, one outside. Only the person key, preferred contact details, consent and a kit stage cross to the commercial side.

Consent rules

  • A consent record has a person, a channel (text, call, email), the address it applies to, a status, a source system and the time it was observed.
  • Status is one of opted out, opted in or unknown. Unknown is never permission: a channel address with no opt-in on record is not messaged for marketing.
  • An opt-out applies to the address it was given for and to the person. After a merge, every address the merged person holds on that channel inherits the opt-out.
  • An opt-in applies only to the address it was given for. It never spreads to other addresses or across a merge.
  • When two people merge and their records conflict for a channel, opted out beats unknown and unknown beats opted in.
  • A split restores each person's own consent records from the event log, then applies any opt-out recorded while they were merged to both people.
  • Consent records are never edited or deleted. A change is a new record, so the history of who was contactable when can be shown.
  • Today's CRM flags sms_opt_out and do_not_call are imported as opted out when true and as unknown when false, because a false flag does not record that anyone agreed.

Controls

  • Encryption in transit and at rest, with identifier columns encrypted at the field level.
  • Role-based access with scopes on the API: commercial consumers can read keys, stages and consent, not identifiers. Reviewers can read identifiers for the two records in front of them. The read is logged.
  • Candidate generation outside the restricted zone uses salted hashes of normalised email and phone, never the values.
  • Logs and error messages carry record IDs and reason codes only. The current code already does this; the rule is kept.
  • Development, testing and AI tooling use synthetic or de-identified data. Nothing from the store is pasted into an assistant or a ticket.
  • Retention follows the clinical record. Merged persons and audit events are kept, not purged, so a wrong merge can be undone.
  • A business associate agreement with every vendor that touches the store, including the hosting and the marketing platform if it receives a results-ready stage.
  • Text messaging honours sms_opt_out per number. A merge can only tighten consent, never loosen it.

What stays as it is

  • E-commerce keeps one customer per email. The identity service absorbs that, so the shop does not need a customer merge feature.
  • The EHR keeps creating charts from name and birthdate. It gains an identifier, not a new workflow.
  • The lab integration is untouched. The kit barcode is already the right key.
  • The CRM's duplicate rule stays, set to alert. It is useful as a signal, harmful as a gate.
  • legacy-sync keeps running in shadow until the new consumer has matched its decisions for a full cycle, then it is retired.
  • Historic CRM data is corrected through identity merges with audit, not edited in place.

Astra 6 adversarial review

Review date: 9 November 2026. Comparison of this target architecture with the Codex future-state design, including the supporting build specification and rollout. This assesses the written proposals; it does not verify that their implementations satisfy them. The recommendations below remain review findings, not changes to the design above.

Recommendation: Use Claude's architecture as the submission's foundation, then incorporate Codex's identity model and result-access safeguards. Claude is more actionable and better connected to this case. Codex draws important boundaries more precisely.

First priority: distinguish unresolved identity from result authorization

The pending-review behavior promises results to the registrant as soon as they exist. That is safe only when patient linkage and the viewer's authority have already been established independently. Being the registrant does not necessarily make someone the patient or an authorized representative. Separate roles are recognized elsewhere in this design, but this promise is too broad.

Codex's result-safety policy requires confirmed patient linkage and normal portal authorization before release. That is the stronger boundary. Its wording should also make clear that an unresolved match to a commercial account cannot delay an otherwise authorized patient viewing their own results.

Proposed combined rule: Accept registration independently of identity resolution. Release results when the patient-to-result linkage and the viewer's authority are established. An unresolved CRM or commerce match does not block that release.

Adopt the more explicit identity-link model

Codex's separate SourceRecord, IdentityLink and MatchEvidence entities distinguish the original record from the current belief about who it belongs to. Link validity dates and superseded decisions make corrections easier to express. Its relationship model includes authority and validity, which is useful when a guardian's access changes.

Claude's direct source-record-to-person mapping can work for this exercise, but recovering history and handling disputed links depends more heavily on the event log. Adopt Codex's separation while retaining Claude's concrete kit roles and readable schema. See the alternative data model.

Keep the operational detail and clarify internal responsibilities

The review workflow accounts for simultaneous reviewers, changing evidence, failed commits and consumers missing updates. The registration specification explains exactly where durability begins. These details make the architecture credible beyond a diagram.

The tradeoff is that the identity service centralizes matching, preferred contacts, consent, kit stages and review. That is reasonable as a small deployment, but these responsibilities should be separated internally. A matcher outage should not stop consent suppression or kit-stage processing.

Further changes before presenting

  • Reconcile the marketing contract. The consumer contract says no stage is sent without the required agreement; the open assumptions permit registered as the fallback. Pick one approved payload and use it throughout.
  • Measure errors after clustering as well as before it. The random audit of automatic links is a good foundation. Add checks for incorrect combined identities: one bad bridge can bring several otherwise correct records into the wrong person. Pair-level precision alone does not describe that impact.
  • Make consent purpose-specific. Keep the conservative opt-out preservation, but incorporate Codex's purpose and scope so marketing preferences and other communication permissions are evaluated explicitly.

For the review session, present Claude's concrete delivery design with Codex's clearer separation of identity, relationships and authorization. Claude is the stronger overall case-study answer; Codex contributes safeguards to incorporate before approving implementation.

Why this page is the stronger answer

The brief says to make a reasonable assumption, write it down and keep going. This page does that: thresholds, a payload per hop, a scoring table, a validation plan and an assumptions table. The Codex page defers the threshold, the transport and the authority model to discovery and ends on seven open questions. One of them asks whether a registration can be accepted before identity resolution, which is the brief's one fixed constraint.

Where this review differs from the one above: the results path

The review above treats Codex's result-safety policy as the stronger boundary. As written, that policy holds a result until the patient link is confirmed. The claim was checked against the CSVs in data/. All 406 lab results carry the same name and birthdate as both their registration and their chart, with every chart holding a single identity. The kit barcode chain already establishes which patient a result belongs to, without the identity service. A hold behind identity review adds a customer-facing delay to the one path that is not broken.

The review above is right on a narrower point: the registrant is not always the patient, so "results are shown to that registrant" is too broad. That is a portal authorization rule, which the portal can enforce without waiting for an identity decision. Both reviews arrive at the same combined rule: release on the kit chain and the viewer's authority, never on an unresolved commercial match. Codex's narrower safeguard is worth keeping as the single exception: stop release when the EHR returns conflicting chart information.

Weak spots in this design

  • Each source record has exactly one current person, so past links live only in the event log. Codex's link entity makes an unmerge and a question such as "who was this record linked to in March" simpler to answer. The changed-requirement part of the review session could probe this.
  • The controls use salted hashes of email and phone for candidate generation. The phone number space is small enough to brute-force. Keyed tokens with the key held in a secrets service are the right answer.
  • Consent has a channel and an address but no purpose. A results notification and a nurture message look the same to the consent rules.
  • "Nothing is deleted" and "kept, not purged" conflict with deletion requests. A consumer test seller may fall under consumer health privacy law, not only HIPAA.
  • A record that scores above the threshold against two different persons bridges them into one unless a cluster rule fires. Codex's single-candidate rule would send that case to review.
  • The sample has two name and birthdate pairs with two charts each. The clinical review path for duplicate charts will get real traffic.

Recommended changes

Keep this page as the submission and port five things from the Codex page.

  1. The link entity with validity dates.
  2. The single-candidate rule for automatic links.
  3. Keyed tokens in place of salted hashes.
  4. Purpose on consent records.
  5. A short paragraph on deletion and data-subject requests.

Add the chart-conflict stop as a narrow exception to "results are never delayed". State the viewer-authority rule for the portal where the registrant and the patient are different people.
