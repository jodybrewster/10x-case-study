Business context and drivers

The business

Lumen Health sells at-home test kits and supplements to consumers. Customers buy online through an e-commerce platform or by phone through sales reps working in a CRM. Every kit ships with a barcode. The customer registers the kit in the member portal and mails a sample to a lab partner. The lab returns a result. It is filed in the EHR and the customer reads it in the portal. Marketing nurtures every customer by stage: bought, registered, results ready.

The four systems each define the customer differently. E-commerce creates a customer per email address. The CRM holds an account matched by an older sync job. The portal keeps the email and details typed at registration. The EHR creates a chart from the registrant name and date of birth. Nothing joins them except the kit barcode, which links e-commerce, the portal and the lab.

Drivers

  • Customers are rejected at the moment of highest intent. Support carries manual moves and merges that are blocked by inactive owners (two inactive reps own 54 of 689 accounts).
  • A text message sent after an opt-out is a compliance exposure as well as a customer-trust one.
  • Health information reaches commercial systems with no audit trail and no access control anywhere in the sync service.
  • The CRM is being replaced. Identity that lives in CRM account IDs and CRM duplicate rules would be rebuilt, with its faults, in the new system. The replacement is a chance to put identity where it can outlive any one CRM.
  • Marketing wants per-kit, per-person stage. A per-account stage cannot tell a second kit that needs registering from a first kit that already has results (70 charts hold more than one result).

The fixed constraint

A customer kit registration is never blocked waiting on identity. This holds from the first week of the rollout and in every later phase. It is the one rule that no other requirement may trade against.

Current state summary

Four systems each keep their own copy of the customer. A small Python service, legacy-sync, copies e-commerce orders and portal kit registrations into the CRM. It decides which CRM account an order or a registration belongs to with three lookups: the e-commerce customer ID, the email address, then last name plus zip code. The service is 365 lines with no third-party dependencies, no logging, no audit trail and no access control.

Orders reach it through a job that handles one order at a time. In the CRM file every opportunity is created 1 to 30 minutes after its order, which fits a polling job on a schedule of about 30 minutes. This is inferred from timestamps. The service writes to the CRM through client calls that are synchronous with no retry, no idempotency key and no transaction. Registration runs inside the customer request: the portal calls register_kit, which has to resolve a CRM account before the kit is accepted, so any CRM failure becomes a failed registration.

Three modules have no caller in the repository: the merge utility, the stage helper and an unfinished fuzzy matcher. They are run by hand or from code that was not supplied. The unfinished matcher should not be adopted: two missing phones or two missing emails count as a match.

The causal chain

The six symptoms are mostly one chain. A hotfix in June started guessing accounts by surname and zip. A change in July started overwriting emails. Registration, added between the two, trusts email alone. Once an order from a relative has overwritten an email, the rightful owner cannot be found by email, the create path is attempted and the duplicate rule rejects the owner as a duplicate of themselves. Marketing then reads a stage from an account that never received the registration.

What else the diagnosis found

  • Registrations can be accepted onto the wrong person silently. When the account birthdate is blank or the placeholder 1920-01-01, the identity check compares surnames only. In the replay 11 registrations were accepted onto an account with a different first name.
  • The first match wins everywhere. 39 registrations matched more than one account by email.
  • Nothing is atomic or idempotent. A registration can create an account and then fail on the move with no rollback. Running an order twice creates two opportunities.
  • A test protects the bug: it asserts that one customer order lands on another customer account and calls it reusing the household account.
  • The address fallback cannot be switched off alone. With it off, 16 orders raise an uncaught duplicate error and 11 kit registrations lose their opportunity. The fallback hides the duplicate rule from the order path.
  • The kit barcode is the one sound join: all 406 lab results carry the registrant name and birthdate, so it is a reliable deterministic key between portal, lab and chart.

Decision

Option D is chosen. It is the only option that scores acceptably on every criterion. It keeps health data in one governed store, survives the CRM replacement, gives reviewers evidence they can read and carries no licence or lock-in cost. It costs engineering time, which is the real price. The design keeps that cost small by limiting the service to identity, roles, consent and review. It is not a general customer data platform.

Where a rejected option is stronger

  • Option A is the cheapest and the quickest. Its Phase 0 fixes are adopted as the first phase of the chosen design because they stop the damage in two weeks. It fails as a destination because identity would still be rebuilt with the CRM replacement.
  • Option B is stronger on mature stewardship tooling, survivorship rules and match analytics out of the box. If the company later outgrows a small service, an MDM product can replace the matcher behind the same events. The cost is months of procurement and a new vendor holding identifiers and, in practice, a golden record that the company would not own in portable form.
  • Option C is stronger on simplicity: one fewer system, one fewer team. If the replacement CRM were permanent and every other system called it for identity, it would be reasonable. The portal and EHR would then depend on the CRM for registration, which is the failure this project exists to remove.
  • Option E is stronger on clinical safety and on duplicate chart handling, because the EHR is the legal record and clinical staff already run an index. It is weaker because commercial data would flow into the clinical system and the schedule would belong to the EHR vendor. The design takes the best of E: duplicate charts go to clinical operations and are merged only in the EHR.

Decision record ADR-01 in section 21 captures this choice.

Solution overview

Summary of the solution

A small identity service sits between the commercial systems and the clinical systems. It gives every real person one opaque person_key and links each record in e-commerce, the CRM, the portal and the EHR to that key with a confidence score and the evidence behind it. Links the service is sure of are made automatically. Uncertain ones go to a review queue for a person to decide. Nothing is ever guessed from a surname and a zip code again.

Each kit carries three roles, buyer, registrant and patient, so a gift or a family purchase no longer has to be forced onto one CRM account. A kit registration is accepted the moment the customer submits it. The service creates a provisional person and resolves it afterwards. Contact details keep their history instead of being overwritten. Consent is recorded per channel address and the strictest setting survives a merge. The CRM and marketing receive the key, the stage of each kit and consent. Nothing clinical crosses to them.

Blue components are new or changed. E-commerce, the portal, the EHR and the lab keep their current behaviour; each gains a field for the person key and an event to the identity service.

End-to-end flows

Each flow is numbered. Steps marked asynchronous happen after the customer has been answered. Every event is idempotent, so a repeated delivery of any step is harmless.

An online order

  1. The customer pays in the e-commerce platform. The platform emits an order event (webhook, or a polled feed with the same shape, an assumption) carrying customer ID, order ID, email, name, phone, shipping name and address and the gift flag.
  2. The identity ingest authenticates the call, stores the event in the inbox keyed on order ID and answers. A repeat of the same order ID returns a duplicate status.
  3. The handler upserts the e-commerce customer as a source record and asks the matcher for candidates. A returning buyer who uses a new email is linked when the other evidence is strong enough. E-commerce has no birthdate, so two customers with the same name and no differing evidence cannot be told from a father and son. Such a pair falls below the automatic threshold unless other records tie it together. Otherwise it goes to review.
  4. The service records the order, the buyer role and the kit barcode when the kit ships. It writes the email to identifier history. The preferred email does not change unless a survivorship rule says so.
  5. Asynchronously, the outbox tells the CRM adapter to create the opportunity on the buyer person with the person_key. Marketing receives stage bought for the kit.

A gift order

  1. The order arrives with the gift flag set. The buyer role is the order customer.
  2. The shipping name becomes a recipient candidate record. It is not linked to any existing person on surname and zip.
  3. The opportunity stays with the buyer. Nothing is moved later as a condition of anything.
  4. When the recipient registers the kit, the registration supplies the registrant and patient roles. Gift registrations follow the registration flow below with no special case.

A kit registration

  1. The customer submits the form. The portal runs four local checks and, in one database transaction, writes the registration and an outbox row. It answers 201. The customer is done.
  2. The relay claims the outbox row and posts the event to the identity ingest. The service stores it in the inbox and acknowledges. The portal now treats the event as delivered.
  3. The handler creates a provisional person for the registrant, writes the registrant and patient roles on the kit and sets the stage to registered. Marketing sees this on its next 15-minute feed.
  4. The matcher resolves the provisional person against existing people. The outcome is merged, new or in review. The portal receives the outcome as a callback and stores the person_key.
  5. The CRM adapter and the EHR key writer are told through the outbox. If the CRM rejects the task it fails on the identity service side and never reaches the customer.

A result arriving

  1. The lab returns a result to the EHR keyed on the kit barcode. This path is unchanged.
  2. The EHR emits a result status event with kit barcode, chart ID and status, without values.
  3. The handler links the registration and the chart deterministically if name and birthdate agree. A disagreement goes to review. The kit stage becomes resulted.
  4. The portal shows the result through the patient role as it does today. Marketing receives stage resulted and stops any registration nudge for that kit.

A marketing send

  1. Every 15 minutes (a proposed interval) the stage feed carries person_key, kit barcode, stage and channel consent for changes since the last run.
  2. Marketing sends "please register" only for a delivered kit with no registration event. It sends nothing on a channel address whose consent is opted out or unknown.
  3. An open review item makes no difference to the stage. Consent is read as the stricter of the two persons for any address they share.

A reviewer decision

  1. The reviewer opens the queue, which lists items by ID, score and reason code. They claim one under a 30-minute lease.
  2. The comparison screen shows the two records. The read of identifiers is logged.
  3. They decide same, different or cannot tell, with a reason from a list. If the merge touches a chart or a result, or crosses surnames, a second reviewer decides without seeing the first answer.
  4. The service re-checks that the evidence is unchanged, then commits merge, consent, event and outbox rows in one transaction. Consumers are told through the outbox.

A wrong-merge correction

  1. A customer, agent or clinician reports a wrong link. This opens a priority 1 item with a 4 working hour target.
  2. A reviewer confirms. A second reviewer confirms if a chart or result is involved.
  3. The service splits the person along the rejected links, restores each side identifiers, links and consent from the event log and stores a keep-apart rule.
  4. The outbox sends a split event to every consumer. If results were visible to the wrong person in the meantime, the privacy officer is notified and the incident process in section 13 starts.

What stays as it is

  • E-commerce keeps one customer per email. The identity service absorbs that, so the shop does not need a customer merge feature.
  • The EHR keeps creating charts from name and birthdate. It gains an identifier, not a new workflow.
  • The lab integration is untouched. The kit barcode is already the right key.
  • The CRM's duplicate rule stays, set to alert. It is useful as a signal, harmful as a gate.
  • legacy-sync keeps running in shadow until the new consumer has matched its decisions for a full cycle, then it is retired.
  • Historic CRM data is corrected through identity merges with audit, not edited in place.

Person key design

The person_key is a ULID: 128 bits made of a 48-bit millisecond timestamp and 80 random bits, written as 26 Crockford base32 characters. It is generated by the identity service when the first record of a person arrives and stored. It is never derived from names, emails, record IDs or any other source data. That rules out guessing a key from a record and means a key cannot change because a lower-sorting record joins the cluster. The timestamp prefix keeps inserts ordered. It reveals only the minting time, which is in the audit log anyway.

A merge keeps one key and sets merged_into on the other. The old key keeps resolving to the survivor forever, so a consumer holding a stale key is never orphaned. A split mints a new key for the divided-off side and leaves the history on the event log.

The batch prototype derives its key as a SHA-256 hash of the smallest member record, shortened to 12 hexadecimal characters. That is a shortcut to make a one-command run repeatable. It is guessable from a record ID and it changes when a lower-sorting record joins a cluster. It is not the target design.

Matching design

Honest status

The batch prototype matches records with hand-set weights. Its scores rank pairs. They are not calibrated probabilities, so a score of 0.95 does not mean a 95 percent chance of being the same person. The 0.90 and 0.60 thresholds are provisional. Precision has not been measured. The prototype draws a seeded random sample for labelling and ships an evaluation script, but nobody has labelled it yet. Everything in this section that depends on a measured rate is a plan until gate G3.

Pipeline in order

  1. Normalise every record (rules in section 9).
  2. Create deterministic links: registration to chart through the lab result for the same kit barcode, only when name and birthdate also agree.
  3. Generate candidate pairs through blocking.
  4. Score each pair, applying vetoes and review-only rules.
  5. Cluster accepted links under constraints.
  6. Group the leftover uncertain pairs into one review item per pair of persons.

Deterministic links first, scored pairs second, people last. The thresholds are set for precision: a wrong merge exposes one person's health information to another, a missed link costs a review.

Deterministic links

  • Registration to chart through the lab result for the same kit barcode. In the sample all 406 results carry the registration's name and birthdate.
  • Order to opportunity through ecom_order_id and order to kit through order_id. These link records, not people: the buyer is the order's customer, the registrant is whoever registered the kit.
  • ecom_customer_id on a CRM account is treated as weak evidence, not a key. The sync stamped it onto relatives' accounts.

Candidate pairs

Two records are compared only when they share a normalised email, a normalised phone, a surname and zip or a surname and birthdate. In the sample this keeps the comparisons in the low thousands instead of the two million possible pairs.

Weights follow the Fellegi-Sunter form: the log ratio of how often a field agrees for true matches against how often it agrees by chance. In the working slice the weights are set by hand, so its scores rank pairs and are not probabilities.

Validation before any automatic link is trusted

  • The unit is the automatic link: a record pair at or above the threshold. The population is every such pair.
  • A simple random sample of that population is labelled independently by two reviewers, with a third settling disagreements. The slice draws this sample with a fixed seed.
  • The false-match rate is reported with a 95 percent Wilson interval, which is valid because the sample is random and complete. The upper bound is held against the gate, never the point estimate.
  • The rollout gate asks for at least 600 labelled automatic links. With no false match in 600 the upper bound is about 0.6 percent. The acceptable bound is a decision for the privacy officer.
  • Samples of the review queue and of pairs just under the threshold are labelled too. They estimate reviewer workload and missed links and say nothing about automatic-link precision.
  • Rule families that have not been measured stay in review whatever they score. Today that covers addresses that match only after a plus tag is removed. Phone numbers with a non-US country code match only on every digit.
  • Weights are refitted from review decisions. The sample is redrawn and relabelled after each refit.

Thresholds

  • 0.90 and above: link automatically, record evidence, raise an event. The 0.90 figure is provisional until the validation above has been run. Until then the service runs in shadow and writes nothing.
  • 0.60 to 0.90: create a review item. Both records remain separate people until decided.
  • Below 0.60: no link, no item.

Clustering

Accepted links are grouped into people with two constraints. A person may not hold two different birthdates. Two EHR charts are never merged by code; a chart pair that scores as one person goes to clinical operations as a review item. Transitive closure runs only across automatic and human-accepted links, never across review items. A record's confidence is the weakest link on its path into the cluster.

What happens while a pair is under review

  • Both records stay separate people. A registration keeps its provisional person, so the kit, its chart and its result remain attached to the registrant and results are shown without delay.
  • The CRM shows the account with a pending-review flag. Nothing is moved.
  • Marketing reads kit stage, which does not depend on the review, so the registrant is not asked to register again.
  • Review items carry reason codes such as same surname and zip, birthdate missing on one side, email shared by two first names. A reviewer sees the two records side by side inside the restricted zone and decides same, different or cannot tell.
  • Every decision is an event and becomes a labelled pair for the next calibration.

Traps the sample data teaches

  • Eight registration emails are used by more than one person and five EHR emails sit on more than one chart. A shared email is a household signal, not an identity.
  • Twenty non-gift kits were registered by the buyer under a second email. Work and personal addresses for one person are common, so a new email must not create a new person when phone, birthdate and name agree.
  • Same-name relatives with different birthdates exist. The birthdate veto handles them where a birthdate is present. Where the CRM has none, the pair goes to review.
  • Consecutive e-commerce customer IDs often belong to one person. That is how the sample was generated, not evidence. The matcher must not use it.

Blocking

Two records become a candidate pair when they share a normalised email, a dot-insensitive email key, a plus-tag base address, a phone, a surname with zip or a surname with birthdate. On the sample this yields comparisons in the low thousands instead of about two million possible pairs. Blocks above 60 records are skipped in the prototype. None occur in the sample. At 100x the surname-and-zip block grows, so production sub-blocks by first initial and counts every skipped block, because a skipped block hides true pairs.

Vetoes and review-only families

  • Veto: two known birthdates that differ. There is no exception for a likely typo or a transposition. A mistyped birthdate costs a review and nothing worse.
  • Veto: a stored keep-apart rule from a human decision, until new evidence arrives.
  • Constraint: two EHR charts are never merged by code.
  • Review only: addresses equal only after a plus tag is removed, because whether a tagged address is the same mailbox depends on the mail provider.
  • Review only: phone numbers with a non-US country code match on every digit and are never auto-linked until measured.
  • Review only: any rule family without enough labelled pairs.

Confidence

A record confidence is the weakest link on its path to the cluster anchor, which is the member the key comes from. The anchor takes its strongest link. A record that is alone in its cluster carries 1.0, which means only that nothing was linked to it, not that it has no duplicate. In the sample 92 records sit at exactly 1.0: the 82 singletons and records joined only by a deterministic link.

Validation plan

The unit of validation is the automatic link: a record pair at or above the threshold. The population is every such pair. The plan has five parts.

  1. Draw a simple random sample of at least 600 automatic links, without replacement, with a fixed seed. The prototype draws 100 of its 2,902 automatic links today and can draw 600.
  2. Two labellers label independently from stored evidence. A third settles disagreements. Disagreement rate is reported because it measures how hard the problem is.
  3. Compute the false-match rate with a 95 percent Wilson interval. Hold the upper bound against the gate, not the point estimate. With no false match in 600 the upper bound is about 0.64 percent. With one it is about 0.94 percent. With three it is about 1.46 percent. Against the proposed gate of under 1 percent, the sample therefore tolerates at most one false match.
  4. Report each rule family separately only where it has enough pairs. Samples for rare families are stratified draws reported on their own and never pooled into the overall interval.
  5. After go-live, draw 2 percent of automatic links at random each day and review them blind, without the matcher score on screen. The audit never stops. A drift in the interval stops automatic linking for the family involved.

Samples of the review queue and of pairs just under the threshold are labelled as well. They estimate reviewer workload and missed links. They say nothing about automatic-link precision.

Recalibration

Closed review items and audit labels become the training set. Weights are refitted in the Fellegi-Sunter form (the log ratio of how often a field agrees for true matches against how often it agrees by chance), on a schedule proposed as monthly. After each refit the thresholds are re-validated on a fresh random sample before the new weights go live. The regression set in section 18 must pass. A reason-code family whose reviewed items come back the same person nearly every time is a candidate for automatic linking. One that comes back different people is a candidate for a stronger negative weight.

Ordering and idempotency

  • Delivery is at least once everywhere. Duplicates are normal and harmless because every event has a unique ID and every state change is idempotent.
  • Global ordering is not promised. Ordering matters per aggregate: events for one registration or one person carry a sequence number and are applied only if higher than the stored one.
  • A registration is unique on registration_id and on kit_barcode. The same kit with the same payload hash replays the first answer. The same kit with a different email is a conflict handled by support. The unique constraint arbitrates simultaneous submissions.
  • A repair or replay reuses the original event_id so it can never produce a second effect.
  • Merges and splits are single transactions that write links, consent, event log and outbox rows together.

API style and versioning

Synchronous interfaces are JSON over HTTPS with the version in the path (/v1). Events carry schema_version with major and minor parts. A schema is closed (additional properties are not allowed) so drift is caught at the edge. A new optional field means a new minor version. The ingest accepts the current and the previous minor of each major. A breaking change means a new major, run side by side for one full rollout window. Errors use a stable reason code and never include a response body from another system or any personal value.

Back-pressure

  • Ingest answers 429 with a retry hint when the inbox depth passes a configured limit. The portal relay treats 429 like a timeout.
  • The relay circuit breaker opens after 5 consecutive failures and sends one probe every 30 seconds. Rows wait in pending and do not use up attempts during an outage.
  • Replay is rate-limited (20 per second to start, raised to 50 only if inbox lag and outbox depth stay flat).
  • The consumer adapters have per-consumer rate limits that respect the CRM and marketing API quotas, which are unknown and assumed.

Process and workflow design

Everything in this section is asynchronous. No customer-facing action waits for a reviewer, a merge or a split. Each process has states, actors, a service level (all proposals, to be set with whoever staffs the queue) and exception paths. For every process the rule on what the systems do while an item is open is the same: an open item is always resolved in the safe direction. Two people are kept apart until someone says otherwise, because a wrong merge exposes health information and a missed merge only delays tidiness.

Safeguards

  • Least exposure. The queue lists items by ID, score and reason code. Names, birthdates and contact details appear only on the comparison screen for the item a reviewer has claimed. That read is logged. Result values are never shown.
  • Blind audit. Audit items hide the matcher's score and decision, so the reviewer is not led.
  • Two-person rule for merges that touch clinical records, as set out above. The second reviewer does not see the first decision.
  • No self-review. A reviewer cannot decide an item about their own record or one they reported.
  • Stale-decision check. When a decision is applied, the service compares the evidence the reviewer saw with the evidence now. If a record, link or birthdate changed in between, the decision is not applied and the item is superseded.
  • Constraints still hold. A human decision cannot create a person with two EHR charts merged by code. A merge that would put two known birthdates on one person requires the reviewer to mark which is the error. It is then routed to a lead.
  • Reversible. Every merge can be split from the event log. Nothing is deleted.
  • Reviewer quality. A share of closed items is re-reviewed by a second person. Agreement is tracked per reviewer.
  • No bulk decisions. There is no approve-all. Decisions are rate-limited per reviewer.

Encryption and key management

  • TLS 1.2 or later on every hop (target 1.3). Storage encryption at rest everywhere.

  • Key rotation yearly and on staff change in the key custodian role (proposed). Key access is logged and alerts on unusual use.
  • Backups use their own keys. A restore drill proves the key chain works.

Consent rules

  • A consent record has a person, a channel (text, call, email), the address it applies to, a status, a source system and the time it was observed.
  • Status is one of opted out, opted in or unknown. Unknown is never permission: a channel address with no opt-in on record is not messaged for marketing.
  • An opt-out applies to the address it was given for and to the person. After a merge, every address the merged person holds on that channel inherits the opt-out.
  • An opt-in applies only to the address it was given for. It never spreads to other addresses or across a merge.
  • When two people merge and their records conflict for a channel, opted out beats unknown and unknown beats opted in.
  • A split restores each person's own consent records from the event log, then applies any opt-out recorded while they were merged to both people.
  • Consent records are never edited or deleted. A change is a new record, so the history of who was contactable when can be shown.
  • Today's CRM flags sms_opt_out and do_not_call are imported as opted out when true and as unknown when false, because a false flag does not record that anyone agreed.

Controls

  • Encryption in transit and at rest, with identifier columns encrypted at the field level.
  • Role-based access with scopes on the API: commercial consumers can read keys, stages and consent, not identifiers. Reviewers can read identifiers for the two records in front of them. The read is logged.
  • Candidate generation outside the restricted zone uses salted hashes of normalised email and phone, never the values.
  • Logs and error messages carry record IDs and reason codes only. The current code already does this; the rule is kept.
  • Development, testing and AI tooling use synthetic or de-identified data. Nothing from the store is pasted into an assistant or a ticket.
  • Retention follows the clinical record. Merged persons and audit events are kept, not purged, so a wrong merge can be undone.
  • A business associate agreement with every vendor that touches the store, including the hosting and the marketing platform if it receives a results-ready stage.
  • Text messaging honours sms_opt_out per number. A merge can only tighten consent, never loosen it.

De-identification for non-production

  • No production identifiers in development, test, staging or AI tooling. Nothing from the store is pasted into an assistant or a ticket.
  • Staging uses a synthetic generator seeded from the shape of the sample data, including relatives with shared surname, zip, phone and email, nicknames and placeholder birthdates.
  • Where a production-shaped extract is needed, a de-identification job replaces names, emails and phones with consistent fakes, shifts each birthdate by a random per-person offset that preserves equality and inequality and randomises street numbers while keeping shared households shared. The job runs inside Z2 and only its output leaves.
  • The supplied sample is itself synthetic. It is treated as PHI anyway, as the brief instructs.

Reliability, performance and capacity

Availability design

The strongest reliability decision is structural: the customer path does not depend on the identity service. The portal accepts a registration on its own database and nothing else. Everything the identity service does for a registration happens afterwards from a durable outbox. The identity service itself is built for a moderate availability target and fast, safe recovery rather than for never being down.

  • Stateless services (ingest and resolve API, workers, relay, review web) run as at least two instances in separate failure zones behind a load balancer.
  • The identity store is a transactional relational database with a synchronous standby in a second zone, automatic failover and point-in-time recovery.
  • The portal outbox shares the portal database, so there is no cross-system transaction and no dual write.
  • No message broker is required at the sampled volumes. The inbox and outbox tables with SKIP LOCKED claims are enough. A broker can be added later behind the same event contract.
  • Every consumer is behind an adapter with its own retry, rate limit and dead-letter handling, so one slow consumer cannot stall another.

Release and change management

  • Trunk-based development with a CI gate: unit tests, contract tests, the matcher regression set, the log scrubber test and a replay of the supplied data.

Migration, cutover and rollout

The rollout has four phases over 90 days. Phase 0 (days 1 to 14) stops the damage in legacy-sync and the registration path without introducing anything new. Phase 1 (days 15 to 45) runs the identity service in shadow mode and proves its matching on a hand-labelled set. Phase 2 (days 46 to 75) switches consumers to the identity result one at a time, each behind a flag. Phase 3 (days 76 to 90) keys the replacement CRM on person_key and retires legacy-sync. The full plan follows. It is also published as the standalone 90-day rollout.

A kit registration is never blocked waiting on identity or on the CRM. That holds from the first week and stays true in every later phase.

Two milestones can look alike and are different. The week 1 to 2 change is an immediate registration-path decoupling: the portal stops depending on the CRM, so a CRM failure can no longer reject a customer. Reliability contract for the registration change

The contract behind "registration never waits" is explicit. The assumption here is that the portal has its own durable store. If it does not, the first task of Phase 0 is to add one.

  • Acceptance boundary: the registration is accepted when the portal has written it to its own store. The customer sees success at that moment.
  • The portal writes a work item in the same transaction as the registration. The registration ID is the idempotency key, so a double submit or a retry creates one record and one CRM action.
  • A worker calls the CRM after acceptance. A rejected call is retried with growing delays. After a fixed number of attempts the item moves to a dead-letter list that support works by hand.
  • Replay: any item or range of items can be re-run by registration ID. A repeated run is safe.
  • If the work queue is unavailable the registration is still stored. A nightly sweep finds stored registrations with no CRM action and creates the missing items.

Clean-up starts on day 60 in batches, after change (d) has shipped and the CRM carries the key. It never edits an account in place.

Data backfill and historic clean-up approach

  1. In Phase 1 the full history of all four systems is loaded in batch into the identity store in shadow mode. The batch pipeline of the prototype is the template. Persons get minted keys. Nothing is written to any production system.
  2. Automatic links are limited to the rule families that passed the validation at G3. Everything else becomes a review item. Items are ordered by priority: pairs where either side holds a result come first.
  3. The recovery of lost data is from the sources. An email the CRM overwrote is rebuilt from e-commerce, portal and EHR history in the identifier table. The old CRM values are not trusted.
  4. Misfiled purchases are found by comparing the opportunity placed by legacy-sync with the buyer person from the identity service. The mismatches become a work list: a role correction or an audited merge or split, never an in-place edit.
  5. From day 60, after the consent merge fix has run in production, clean-up proceeds in batches. Each batch is sampled before it is applied and applies only if the sample shows no false merge. Batches are paced to queue age.

Mapping the old CRM account_id to person_key

The mapping is built from the identity store and kept after the cutover. It has one row per old account: account_id, person_key, confidence, method and a status of mapped, in review or retired. Rules:

  • Every account maps to the person of its source_record. Two old accounts that the service merged map to the same person_key. The replacement CRM creates one account for that person and keeps both old IDs in a legacy_account_ids field so old references still resolve.
  • An account in the review queue is listed as in review and does not move to the replacement CRM as a person until it is decided. Its data is carried over as a separate pending person so no record is lost.
  • An account that holds a purchase of a different person is carried over with the purchase placed on the buyer person, using the role correction from clean-up.
  • The replacement CRM takes person_key as a required external ID. This is a dependency to confirm early (section 22).
  • Completeness test before cutover: accounts equal mapped plus in review plus retired, with a sampled hand check of the mapping.

Decommissioning legacy-sync

  1. Day 61: legacy-sync moves to shadow after G5. It keeps its state so it can be switched back on.
  2. Run 14 consecutive days (assumed to be one full cycle, possibly longer if production volume justifies a month end) with every daily difference explained.
  3. At G8 on day 90: freeze, run a final comparison, turn off the scheduler and the integration user credentials, archive the code, history and logs read-only. The supplied history is evidence and is kept.
  4. Write the restore procedure for the last release. Remove flags and the comparison job.
  5. Thirty days after retirement, remove the runtime infrastructure if nothing has needed it.

Test data policy

Synthetic data only outside Z2. The supplied sample is synthetic and is still treated as PHI. Staging uses a generator that reproduces the hard shapes: relatives with shared surname, zip, phone and email, nicknames, placeholder and missing birthdates, work and personal emails for one person, gifts to the buyer household and to another household. A de-identification job can produce a production-shaped extract inside Z2. Nothing real is pasted into an assistant, a ticket or a test fixture.

Matcher regression set

  • Seeded from the labelled pairs: the 600 or more automatic links, the review sample and the near-threshold sample after labelling.
  • Every wrong merge and every wrong split from production adds a pair. Every hard case (reviewer disagreement) adds a pair.
  • Each pair has an expected decision (auto, review or reject) and the reason codes it must carry. A change fails if it moves any expected-different pair to auto.
  • The set has a protected subset of known traps: father and son with the same name, shared email between relatives, one person with a work and a personal email, nickname pairs, a transposed birthdate.

Contract tests per interface

Each interface in the catalogue has a provider test and a consumer test against the same schema. Tests assert the required fields, the closed property list, the idempotency key behaviour (a second delivery changes nothing), the error codes and the absence of any field the consumer must not receive.

TermMeaning
